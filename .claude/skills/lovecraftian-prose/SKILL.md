# Dunsanian prose

The voice contract for the **frame and the myth layer**: the 2042 interludes, the prologue and epilogue, Ledger clauses, nested tales, rumours and Age blurbs.

**Not for event bodies.** Individual events, outcome text and character blurbs are plain and concrete — **rothfuss-prose**. Applying elevated diction to them is register bleed, and it is a listed failure mode.

Architecture — what an Age is *for*, the escalation stages, the two antagonist tiers, the two-class ledger, which motifs darken and when — is **eldritch-story**. This file is only about how the words go.

## The effect

> **A beautiful myth, told by someone who has glimpsed something behind the myth.**

Ornate, dreamlike, melancholy, and slightly unreliable. Not archaic. The narrator sounds ancient and informed and not entirely trustworthy — a person recounting something old that they only partly understand.

---

## 1. Elevated diction, not archaic diction

Favour words with ceremonial weight: *beneath, beyond, upon, ancient, forgotten, desolate, wondrous, terrible, magnificent, melancholy, elder, accursed, innumerable, unremembered, silent.*

**Never** use dead grammar: `ere`, `mayhap`, `betwixt`, `whilst`, `'twas`, `forsooth`, `thee`. Age is carried by **weight and cadence**, not by a register that stopped being spoken four hundred years ago. The line should feel literary, not costumed.

The test: read it aloud. If it sounds like someone at a lectern, it is right. If it sounds like a renaissance fair, cut back.

---

## 2. Long, accumulating sentences

Let clauses gather. This is the single loudest difference from the Rothfuss register, where the instruction is the opposite.

> Beyond the last of the family's holdings, where the tenants said the river had once run the other way and the older ones would not say why, there stood a house whose foundations were older than the name above its door.

Not:

> There was a house beyond the fields. It was very old. Its foundations were ancient.

**Short sentences are punctuation, not default.** One per paragraph, at most, and placed where it lands hardest. The long line is the register; the short line is the strike.

---

## 3. Mythic narrative distance

The narrator recounts rather than reports. Reach for:

- *It is said…*
- *There are those who tell…*
- *The oldest of the family's books relate…*
- *Few now remember…*
- *In the years before…*
- *Whether this is true, the chronicle does not say…*
- *And yet…*
- *There are matters concerning which even the record is silent.*

**Do not overuse.** Three or four per interlude is already too many; at that density the device stops creating distance and starts creating fog. One well-placed *few now remember* does more than four.

The narrator should be **ancient, knowledgeable, and slightly unreliable** — the last of those is the point, because in this game the myth layer is the layer that lies.

---

## 4. Beauty, and its temporariness

The register's default emotional colour is **immense beauty accompanied by sadness**. The beautiful thing should be passing, or already past, or about to be.

Write beauty in the same sentence as its loss:

> The hall was full that year, and would not be full again for three generations, and nobody in it knew that.

The sadness is structural rather than decorative — it is what gives the later dread somewhere to land. (Why this matters at design scale: **eldritch-story** §9.)

---

## 5. Describing the incomprehensible

**The less comprehensible a thing is, the less literally it should be described.** This is the register's hardest instruction and its most important one.

Reach for: metaphor · absence · sensory contradiction · impossible geometry · distorted time · incomplete perception · the reactions of witnesses · the state of the room afterward.

> He saw something at the end of the table, though *something* is a word that assumes a great deal.

> It had no face, and it was looking at him.

> The candles behind it were not blocked, but seemed to have forgotten that they had ever been lit.

> The door was both at the end of the hall and had never been built.

**Do not explain how.** Contradiction that gets resolved was never contradiction; it was a riddle.

### Never write the unknowable as a person

No stated intent, no plans, no grievances. The moment a thing announces what it wants, it is a villain, and a villain is comprehensible.

> Never: *"I shall have what I am owed."*
>
> Prefer: *It turned toward the box, though the box had been behind it a moment before, and every clock in the house lost the same four minutes.*

Register an event with no motive attached. Let the reader supply the dread.

---

## 6. What to avoid

**Genre furniture used because the genre has it:** gore, mutilation, screaming monsters, conventional demons, excessive tentacles, explicit violence, madness-as-shorthand, cults, forbidden books. Use any of them only when a specific scene genuinely needs it, never as atmosphere.

**Prose faults this register is prone to:**

- adjective stacking — *the vast, ancient, terrible, silent hall*
- three ornate sentences in a row with no short line to break them
- mythic-distance phrases used as filler between actual content
- explaining the metaphor in the sentence after the metaphor
- modern conversational phrasing that punctures the atmosphere
- generic fantasy description that could belong to any world

---

## 7. Revising

Read it aloud. Then, in order:

1. **Find the archaism** and replace it with a plain word of equal weight.
2. **Find the flat stretch** — three long sentences of similar shape — and put a short one in it.
3. **Cut one adjective from every stack of three.**
4. **Count the mythic-distance phrases.** Keep the best one per paragraph.
5. **Find the thing you explained** and delete the explanation.
6. **Check the incomprehensible was described by effect, not by feature.** If it has a shape, a size and a colour, it is a monster.
7. **Check there is beauty in it.** A passage of unbroken dread is a passage the reader stops minding.

---

## 8. On imitation

Rhythm, sensory ordering, mythic distance and description-by-absence are **techniques**, and techniques are free.

The **furniture** is not: named characters, invented terms, coined proverbs, actual sentences from Dunsany or Lovecraft. If a phrase would make a reader of either nod in recognition, it is a quotation — cut it.

Express the influence through cosmic scale, epistemic uncertainty, dangerous knowledge and the inadequacy of human language, never through imitation of specific passages.
