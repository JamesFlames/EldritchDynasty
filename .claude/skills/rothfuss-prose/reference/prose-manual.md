# Writing Prose in the Style of Patrick Rothfuss

A sentence- and paragraph-level manual. This document is about **how the words go**. For architecture, cast, magic, and plot, see `.claude/skills/eldritch-story/reference/story-manual.md`.

This manual governs **events**. The frame, interludes, prologue, Ledger clauses, nested tales and Age blurbs use an elevated Dunsanian register — see `.claude/skills/lovecraftian-prose/SKILL.md`.

All example sentences below marked *(original)* are written for this guide in the target style — use them as models, not as quotations. The few short phrases attributed to Rothfuss are quoted for diagnostic purposes only.

> **On imitation.** Style is not property, and none of what follows is. Rhythm, structure, sensory ordering, and the habit of describing a room by what is missing from it are techniques, and techniques are free. What is *not* free is the furniture: named characters, invented terms, coined proverbs, and the actual sentences of the books. If a phrase in your draft would make a Rothfuss reader nod in recognition, it is a quotation. Cut it.

### How to use this document

Sections 0–8 are the craft. Read them once, in order, before drafting. Sections 9–17 are lookup — go to the one you need while writing. Sections 18–22 are for revision, and §20 is a procedure to be followed in order rather than a list to browse.

| Part | Sections | What it covers |

## 0. The Core Principle

> **Plain words, arranged musically, about physical things — then one turn into feeling, then stop.**

Rothfuss is not a difficult writer. His vocabulary is mostly short, concrete, and Anglo-Saxon. The lyricism comes almost entirely from **rhythm, repetition, structure, and where he chooses to stop** — not from ornate diction. Writers imitating him usually fail by reaching for the thesaurus. The style is closer to a folk ballad than to purple fantasy.

If a sentence in your draft could not be spoken aloud in a room, comfortably, by a man telling a story to two people, it is not in this style.

## 1. The Two Voices

You must be able to write both, and switch cleanly.

### Voice A — The Frame Voice
Third person, past tense, present-day scenes. **Cool, hushed, elegiac, patient.** Long sentences built from short clauses. Heavy on absence, weight, stillness, and things that are not happening. No jokes. Almost no dialogue tags beyond *said*. Verbs of state (*lay, was, sat, held, waited*) do the work that action verbs would do elsewhere.

> *(original)* The road outside was empty, and had been empty for hours. Inside, the fire had burned down to a red memory of itself, and the innkeeper let it. He had cloths to fold. He folded them slowly, and set each one atop the last, and did not look at the door.

### Voice B — The Telling Voice
First person, past tense, the life story. **Warm, quick, wry, confiding, performative.** He is speaking aloud to people in a room. He addresses the reader, digresses on purpose, admits to editing, and is funny. Rhythms are more varied and faster.

> *(original)* I should say something about the roof of the Mews, since I spent a good portion of my second term on it. It was flat, it was warm, and no one else knew the way up. For a boy with no money and a great deal of pride, that made it something close to a palace.

**Switch rule:** the frame is always *quieter* and *slower* than the tale. If your interludes are as energetic as your story, you've lost the contrast that makes the structure work.

**If your project has more than one teller** — several chroniclers, several documents, a story handed between narrators — these two are the poles, not the whole set. See §17 for building distinct voices between them.

## 2. Sentence Rhythm: The Load-Bearing Technique

### 2.1 The long–long–short cadence
The signature Rothfuss paragraph shape. Two or three sentences (or clauses) of accumulating length and detail, then a short one that lands.

> *(original)* He had a good voice for it, low and unhurried, the sort of voice that makes you believe the ending before you hear it. He sang about a farmer's daughter and a soldier and a road that ran between them, and he made the road sound longer than it was. Everyone in the room went quiet. Even the drunks.

The short final sentence is the entire trick. **Never explain it. Never soften it. Never follow it with another sentence doing the same job.**

Two-word and three-word closers are permitted and powerful: *Even the drunks. It was not. Nothing came. I did.*

### 2.2 The long sentence made of short pieces
Rothfuss's long sentences are almost never subordinated architecture. They are **short units strung with commas and *and***, which is why they sound spoken rather than written.

- Bad (not the style): *Having failed to secure the necessary funds, and being unwilling to approach Devi again so soon, I resolved upon a course of action which, in retrospect, seems ill-advised.*
- Good *(original)*: *I did not have the money. I could not ask Devi again, not so soon, not after the last time. So I did the stupid thing, and I did it quickly, before I could think better of it.*

### 2.3 Vary hard
Count your sentence lengths across a paragraph. You want a jagged profile: 18, 9, 24, 4. Not 15, 16, 14, 17. A run of same-length sentences is the most common tell of failed imitation.

This is measurable, and worth measuring — see §20, pass 4. It is also the failure that assembled or templated text falls into by construction; see §16.9.

### 2.4 The fragment
Used sparingly, always for emphasis, usually after a full sentence.

> *(original)* Nothing in the room had moved. Not the dust. Not the light.

## 3. The Rule of Three

Rothfuss's most recognisable structural habit, at every scale. The prologue of *The Name of the Wind* announces "a silence of three parts" and then delivers three. The world's proverbs run "There are three things all wise men fear."

### 3.1 The announced triad
State the number, then deliver exactly that number, in ascending order of weight. The third item is always the largest, the strangest, or the one that hurts.

> *(original)* There were three things in the room that did not belong. The first was the lamp, too fine for a house like that. The second was the smell of new iron, faint under the smoke. The third was the silence, which was not the silence of an empty house, but the other kind.

### 3.2 The unannounced tricolon
Three parallel items in a single sentence, usually short-short-longer.

> *(original)* She had a low voice, a quick hand, and a way of looking at you that made you check your pockets afterward.

### 3.3 The folk formulation
World-proverbs in the form *There are three things...* / *A man can live on X, Y, and Z, but...* Use these as the ambient wisdom style of your world — they make aphorism feel cultural rather than authorial.

### 3.4 Discipline
Three triads on one page is too many. Use the announced triad for **openings, chapter ends, and moments of ritual weight** only. Elsewhere, use pairs, or use four items to deliberately break the pattern when you want a scene to feel wrong.

## 4. Description by Absence

The most distinctive Rothfuss move, and the easiest to adopt: **describe a place by what is missing from it.**

The prologue does this at length — the lack of wind, the lack of a crowd, the lack of music — using constructions like *"If there had been music… but no, of course there was no music."*

Patterns to use:

**The negative catalogue.**
> *(original)* There was no fire in the grate, no bread on the board, no boots by the door. There was a chair pulled up close to the cold hearth, as if someone had sat there a long while, waiting for it to catch.

**The conditional that refuses itself.**
> *(original)* If there had been a dog, it would have barked. But there was no dog, and had not been for some time.

**The thing that is not there being more present than the things that are.**
> *(original)* Everything was in its place. That was the trouble with it.

Use absence for grief, aftermath, and the frame voice. Use presence and sensory abundance for the telling voice, especially food, warmth, music, and company.

## 5. Diction

### 5.1 Keep it short and physical
Prefer *stone, iron, bread, cold, rain, hand, road, coin, dark, wind, salt, wool, smoke* over abstractions. When Rothfuss wants beauty he reaches for a **common word in an uncommon position**, not a rare word.

- Not: *a coruscating, ineffable luminosity*
- Yes *(original)*: *a light so thin you could have read a letter by it, but only a short one*

### 5.2 Adverbs
Low. Strong verb over verb+adverb, nearly always. *He walked quickly* → *He hurried.* Exception: adverbs of manner in dialogue-adjacent narration where the rhythm needs a soft third syllable. Trust the ear over the rule.

### 5.3 Verbs
Concrete and often quiet. In the frame voice, prefer verbs of state and slow process (*lay, settled, held, sank, waited, guttered, cooled*). In the telling voice, prefer quick physical verbs (*caught, snapped, ducked, spent, ran, lied*).

### 5.4 Never reach for the archaic
No *whilst, ere, betwixt, mayhap*. The world's antiquity is carried by **content and proverb**, not by pseudo-medieval grammar. Rothfuss's narrator sounds like a bright, modern, well-read speaker telling an old story.

### 5.5 Made-up words
Allowed, and load-bearing in most fantasy projects. The rules are involved enough to have their own section — see §14.

## 6. Figurative Language

### 6.1 The homely vehicle
Similes reach for the domestic and tactile, not the cosmic. Compare unfamiliar things to bread, cloth, tools, weather, animals, and rooms.

> *(original)* His anger was banked and quiet, the way a fire is quiet under an hour of ash.
> *(original)* The lie sat between us like a third person at the table, eating nothing.

### 6.2 The extended structural metaphor
Occasionally, take a metaphor and *build* on it for a whole paragraph or a whole scene — the way the prologue treats silence as a substance with parts, an alloy, a counterpoint. This is where the "prose poem" quality comes from. Reserve it for openings, endings, and the two or three most important emotional beats in a book.

### 6.3 Music as the master metaphor
Sound imagery is privileged above all other senses. Non-musical things are described in musical terms, and music is described in physical and emotional terms rather than technical ones.

> *(original)* The room had the sound a room gets when everyone in it has decided not to say the same thing.
> *(original)* I played it badly at first, and then less badly, and then somewhere in the third verse the song stopped being something I was doing and became something that was happening.

**Rule for writing music:** never describe notes, keys, or technique. Describe (a) what it costs the player, (b) what happens in the room, (c) what it does to one specific listener. Lengthen the scene deliberately — slow the cadence, shorten the sentences, let paragraphs get quieter as the music gets better.

### 6.4 The signature closing image
The best Rothfuss lines pair a plain noun with an unexpected qualifier to make a compound that shouldn't work and does — the prologue's "the patient, cut-flower sound of a man who is waiting to die" is the canonical example: a sound described as a *flower*, and the flower is *cut*, and therefore dying beautifully in a vase.

Construct these deliberately: take an abstract state (waiting, shame, hope), pick a **concrete object with a built-in fate** (cut flowers, a banked fire, a coin worn smooth, ice on a step at dawn), and fuse them.

> *(original)* It was the careful, borrowed-coat kind of politeness, the sort you can hear a man doing.

**Budget them.** Two or three per chapter, no more. The compound image is the most conspicuous move in the style and the fastest to curdle. When every paragraph closes on one, they stop being images and become a mannerism.

## 7. Sensory Hierarchy

Rank your senses in this order and you will sound like him:

1. **Sound** (and silence, its inverse) — including the *quality* of a silence
2. **Touch and temperature** — cold hands, warm bread, splinters, the weight of stone
3. **Smell** — smoke, rain on dust, cheap tallow, a woman's hair
4. **Sight** — used mostly for light quality, never for cataloguing
5. **Taste** — reserved for food scenes, which should be loving and specific

Avoid the fantasy-default of describing everything visually. When you enter a new place, give **one sound, one temperature, one smell** before you give a single visual detail.

> *(original)* The stairwell smelled of cold stone and old lamp oil. It was ten degrees colder than the hall behind me, and my footsteps came back off the walls a half-beat late.

## 8. Paragraph Architecture

The standard Rothfuss paragraph has four moves. Not every paragraph uses all four, but the shape is consistent:

1. **The plain opening statement.** Short. Declarative. Sets the frame.
2. **The expansion.** Two or three sentences of concrete, sensory, slightly accumulating detail.
3. **The turn.** A shift from the physical to the emotional, or from the general to the personal, often signalled by *But*, *Still*, *And yet*, or a sudden *I*.
4. **The landing.** A short sentence, plain, that carries the feeling and does not explain it.

> *(original)*
> Ambrose was waiting for me at the bottom of the stairs. *(opening)*
> He had two of his friends with him, and all three of them had the loose, easy posture of young men who have never once had to run. One of them was eating an apple. He kept eating it the whole time. *(expansion)*
> I want to say I was frightened, and I was, a little. But mostly I was doing arithmetic. I had four jots to my name, three days until term fees, and a lute that could not be replaced. *(turn)*
> So I smiled at him and said nothing at all. *(landing)*

**Paragraph length:** mostly short. Three to six sentences. Long blocks are reserved for lyrical set pieces (music, grief, a description of a place that matters).

**The landing must be earned.** It carries feeling the expansion built; it cannot manufacture feeling on its own. A short final sentence attached to a paragraph that did no work reads as a writer hoping for weight. When in doubt, land on a plain fact rather than a plain emotion — the fact will do more.

## 9. The Narrator's Toolkit (Telling Voice Only)

These are the moves that make the first-person voice feel like a person talking, and they are heavily used.

### 9.1 Direct address
> *(original)* You have to understand what a lute is worth to a boy like that.
> *(original)* I know how this sounds. Bear with me.

### 9.2 Admitted editing
The narrator openly curates. This is the seam where prose style meets the book's theme of story vs. truth.
> *(original)* There is more to tell about that winter, but most of it is only cold and hunger, and one telling is enough.
> *(original)* I will skip ahead. What happened at the trial is a story of its own, and not a good one.

### 9.3 The foreshadow-and-refuse
> *(original)* I did not know it then, but that was the last easy night I would have for a long while.

### 9.4 The self-correction
Gives the sense of live speech and of a man reaching for accuracy.
> *(original)* She was beautiful. No — that is the lazy word, and she deserves better. She was *interesting* to look at, which lasts longer.

### 9.5 The aphorism
Short, balanced, folk-flavoured, and delivered as if quoted from the world rather than invented by the speaker. Often attributed to the lost mentor.
> *(original)* Ben used to say that a man who is not afraid of fire has never had to build one in the rain.

**Discipline:** at most one aphorism per two or three pages, and never two in a row. Overused, they become fortune cookies — the most common failure of Rothfuss imitation.

### 9.6 Rueful retrospect
The adult narrator judging his younger self, gently and specifically. This is the primary anti-arrogance valve in the voice.
> *(original)* I was fifteen, and I thought this was cleverness. It was not cleverness. It was luck wearing cleverness's coat.

## 10. Humour

The telling voice is funny, and imitators almost always drop this. A book written entirely in the elegiac register is exhausting by page sixty. Humour is not decoration here — it is what buys the reader's patience for the slow chapters and makes the grief land when it comes.

The comedy is dry, specific, and structural. It is never a joke arriving as a joke.

### 10.1 The deflating specific
A grand statement, punctured by one small concrete fact.
> *(original)* I had decided that morning to become the sort of man who is afraid of nothing. By noon I was standing very still in a cooper's yard, hiding from a woman who was owed four mites.

### 10.2 Understatement after disaster
The size of the event and the size of the sentence do not match. The gap is the joke.
> *(original)* The fire took the roof, both looms, and most of my reputation as a careful person.

### 10.3 The list that goes wrong on the third item
Uses the rule of three against itself. The reader is trained to expect the third item to be the heaviest; give them the stupidest instead.
> *(original)* He had three great virtues. He was strong, he was loyal, and he had never in his life been troubled by a second thought.

### 10.4 The bystander who will not stop
A mundane action continued through a tense scene, never remarked on. The apple in §8 is this technique; it does more for that paragraph than any adjective could.

### 10.5 Banter: agreement is the enemy
Friends do not agree, they amend. Give one friend the habit of taking a figure of speech literally, and another the habit of answering the question nobody asked.
> *(original)* "It's a simple plan," Wil said.
> "It has three parts," I said.
> "Then it is a simple plan," said Sim, "in whatever country you're from."

### 10.6 Rueful retrospect as comedy
See §9.6. The narrator is the safest target in the book, and the only one who can be mocked without cost.

**Rules:**
- **Never signal.** No *hilariously*, no *I couldn't help but laugh*, no character laughing to tell the reader something was funny.
- **Land and stop.** A joke obeys §2.1 exactly: build, then a short sentence, then nothing.
- **Punch inward.** The narrator, his pride, and his arithmetic are fair game. A character the reader is meant to love is not.
- **Place it before the dark, not after.** Humour buys patience in advance. A joke immediately after a death spends credit you needed.
- **Warmth over wit.** If a line is clever but makes the room colder, cut it. The purpose of the comedy is to make the reader want to stay in the tavern.

## 11. Dialogue

- **Tags:** *said* and *asked*, almost exclusively. Occasionally no tag at all for fast exchanges.
- **Beats over adverbs:** replace *he said angrily* with a physical beat. *(original)* — *"Fine." He set the cup down with more care than the cup required.*
- **Wit is the register.** Friends bicker, understate, and undercut each other. Banter is a load-bearing part of the warmth. See §10.5.
- **Clipped and short.** Most lines are under twelve words. Speeches are rare and reserved for characters performing a tale.
- **The silence beat.** Rothfuss uses non-answers constantly. Someone asks a hard question; the answer is an action, or nothing.
  > *(original)* "Did you love her?"
  > I turned the glass around on the bar, once, and did not answer, and after a while he stopped waiting.
- **Distinct speech shapes:** give each recurring character one syntactic habit (never finishes sentences; always answers a question with a question; uses your name in every line). This does more than dialect spelling ever will. Avoid phonetic accent rendering.

---

## 12. Handling Time

- **Summary is a feature, not a failure.** The telling voice compresses freely: *(original)* — *The next two months were a blur of rope, rain, and other people's cargo. I will not pretend I remember them well.*
- **Then slow all the way down** for the scene that matters. The contrast between compressed months and a fully rendered five minutes is a core rhythm of the books.
- **Chapter-scale rhythm:** short chapter, one scene, one turn. Let a very short chapter (500 words) land after a long one.

---

## 13. Punctuation and Mechanics

- **Comma-and chaining** is the workhorse for spoken rhythm: *He came in, and he sat down, and he did not order anything.*
- **Em dashes** for interruption, self-correction, and the reaching-for-the-right-word move. Use freely but not decoratively.
- **Semicolons: rare.** They read as written rather than spoken. When you want one, use a period.
- **Colons: rare in narration**, occasionally used to introduce an announced triad or a list.
- **Italics** for emphasis on a single word, for invented terms on first use, for song lyrics, and for the true names of things. Do not italicise whole sentences.
- **Numbers and prices spelled out** and used constantly — money is texture as well as tension.
- **Sentence-initial *And* / *But* / *So*** are entirely welcome; they are the spoken register's connective tissue.

## 14. Invented Vocabulary

Most fantasy projects carry a heavy load of invented terms — coins, ranks, institutions, disciplines, magical categories. The Rothfuss approach is to teach them the way a language is learned rather than the way a manual is read.

### 14.1 Never define on first use
Put the word in a sentence where context does most of the work, and let the rest stay unpaid for a while. Readers tolerate partial understanding far longer than writers believe.

> *(original)* He paid the man in drabs, and counted them out slowly, which told everyone at the table what he thought of him.

The reader now knows a drab is a small coin, that paying in them is an insult, and that the speaker noticed. No definition was given.

### 14.2 Gloss by consequence, not by apposition
The apposition gloss is the loudest tell of amateur fantasy.

- Not: *a drab, the smallest coin in the Commonwealth*
- Yes: *two drabs would not buy bread, and three would not buy good bread*

Show what it costs, what it opens, who is allowed to hold it, or who is ashamed to have it. A term the reader has seen *used* three times is better installed than one they have seen defined once.

### 14.3 The mouth-feel test
Say the word aloud twenty times. If you stumble, so will the reader, every time, forever. Common things get one or two syllables. Length is a luxury reserved for the rare and the ceremonial — the ritual can be polysyllabic precisely because it happens twice in the book.

**No apostrophes.** No mid-word capitals. Invented words should look like they have etymologies and have been worn down by use.

### 14.4 Density cap
At most **one new term per scene**, and no more than **three unglossed terms live at once**. When a fourth arrives, one of the earlier three must have been used enough times to be furniture. Exceeding this reliably produces the effect where readers skim proper nouns, which is fatal — because two chapters later you need them to care about one.

### 14.5 Let the terms disagree
The same thing called different names by different classes, regions, or institutions is worth more than a glossary. What the scholars call one thing, the people in the village call another, and neither will use the other's word. This is depth for free, and it makes the world feel argued-over rather than issued.

### 14.6 Let the narrator have an opinion about the word
> *(original)* The Masters call it *sufficiency*, which is a word chosen by men who have never had to be sufficient at anything.

This does three jobs at once: it teaches the term, it characterises the narrator, and it makes the institution feel like a thing with a history and a smugness.

### 14.7 Write as if there is no appendix
There may be a glossary; assume nobody opens it. Every term must be survivable from the text alone. If your beta readers reach chapter ten without knowing what something costs, the fault is in the prose, not in their attention.

*(In* The Slow Regard of Silent Things*, Rothfuss goes much further, inventing a private lexicon for a single character's perception — a legitimate advanced move when the POV justifies it, and only then.)*

## 15. Writing Short

Most of this manual assumes runway. Much real work does not have it: a passage of eighty to two hundred and fifty words, read on a screen, standing alone. Short-form is where imitations of this voice fail hardest, because the writer tries to fit every technique into a space that holds one.

**At passage scale you have room for one technique and a landing.**

- **Choose the one.** Absence, *or* a triad, *or* an extended image, *or* a compression of time. Never two. Two techniques in a hundred words reads as a writer showing their work.
- **The landing is not optional.** It is the only thing that makes a hundred and twenty words sound like this voice instead of like competent flat prose. Everything else can go; that cannot.
- **Open on the physical, in the first sentence.** No establishing clause. No weather report. No *It was the third month of the year when—*.
- **One sensory channel**, and make it sound or temperature.
- **Aphorisms: almost never.** At this length an aphorism is a tenth of the text and reads as a fortune cookie.
- **Announced triads: only if the passage *is* the triad.** Otherwise there is no room to make the third item land.
- **Cut the first sentence.** Most short passages begin one sentence before they should. Delete it and read again; four times in five the passage is better.

Worked example, 76 words — absence, then a landing, then nothing:

> *(original)* The wet nurse would not come back the second week, and she would not say why, and the money did not change her mind.
>
> They found another in Tarn, older, who asked no questions and slept with the door open. The child was quiet. That was the trouble with him. A baby that does not cry is a baby that has decided something.

Note what is not there: no adjectives of mood, no aphorism, no triad, no simile. One technique. One landing. Out.

## 16. Voice Under Variation

For text that is assembled rather than written straight through — templates with substituted names, numbers, and pronouns; optional clauses; fragments joined at runtime. The voice survives this, but only if the prose is built for it from the first draft. Retrofitting is much harder than authoring correctly.

**16.1 Never let a variable carry the landing.** The final short sentence must be fixed text. *He was gone.* survives; *{name} was gone.* dies the moment the name is five syllables. If the landing must reference the subject, use a pronoun or a role noun — *the boy*, *the man*, *her husband*.

**16.2 Put variables early.** A length change costs least at the head of a sentence and most at the tail. Never place a substitution in the last three words of a paragraph.

**16.3 Write to both extremes.** Test every template with the shortest and the longest legal value. If either version limps when read aloud, restructure — do not tune for the average case, because the average case is not what ships.

**16.4 Never put a variable inside a triad.** Three items must be balanced by the author, and any substitution unbalances them. If a triad must contain generated content, generate all three from the same source with matched lengths, or don't use a triad.

**16.5 Numbers must be magnitude-safe.** *It came to more than he had* survives every value. *It came to eleven crowns, which was a fortune* is wrong when the value is three mites. If the sentence must comment on size, branch the sentence — do not write one sentence and hope.

**16.6 Avoid pronoun chains.** Use the name once, then restructure to avoid three clauses of agreement. Never write a sentence whose joke or rhythm depends on gender.

**16.7 Optional clauses must be rhythm-neutral.** If removing a clause turns a twenty-two-word sentence into an eight-word one, you have written two different paragraphs and should ship two.

**16.8 Own the seams.** Where two authored fragments join, the join always shows at the connective. Begin the second fragment with a hard full stop and a new subject — never with a pronoun or a *which* reaching back into text you did not write.

**16.9 Author paragraphs, not sentences.** Assembling text sentence-by-sentence from a pool produces a uniform length profile by construction, which is exactly the failure in §2.3. Rhythm is a property of the paragraph. Make the paragraph the unit of authorship, and let variation happen between paragraphs.

**Worked pair:**

> Bad: *{child} was {age} years old when {he/she} awakened, and the family was very happy, and {head} declared a feast that cost {n} crowns.*

Everything here is wrong: three variables in one sentence, a pronoun branch, a number the prose comments on, no landing, flat rhythm, and a stated emotion.

> Good *(original)*: *It happened in the pantry, of all places, and it happened at night, and the house took both of these as signs and argued about them for thirty years. {Child} was {age}. Nobody had thought to watch for it that early. There was a feast, and it cost more than the house could afford, and nobody wrote down the number.*

The variables sit in a short fixed frame in the middle. The landing is fixed text. The cost is handled without a figure — which also happens to be better writing than a figure would have been.

## 17. Many Narrators

For projects where the telling voice is not one person: several chroniclers, documents in different hands, a story passed along. The goal is that a reader can identify the teller from three sentences with no attribution.

### 17.1 Differentiate by attention and silence first
Two narrators who **notice different things** read as two people even in identical grammar. Two narrators who use different grammar but notice the same things read as one person doing accents. Grammar is the last dial you turn, not the first.

### 17.2 The voice sheet
One of these per narrator, filled in before they write a word:

| Dial | Range |
|---|---|
| **Attention** | What they see first: money, bodies, weather, God, other people's faces, the room |
| **Silence** | What they will not write down, ever, and what they write instead |
| **Person** | *I* / *we* / *the family* / impersonal |
| **Mean sentence** | Short and blunt / medium / long and chained |
| **Connective** | *and*-chaining / *but*-turning / no connectives at all / subordination (rare, and marks a trained writer) |
| **Register** | Plain, formal, sly, devotional, aggrieved |
| **Tic** | One syntactic habit. Used no more than twice per passage |

**Warning on tics:** a tic is a signature, not a personality. If the tic is doing all the work, the voice is a costume. Fill in Attention and Silence properly and you will find you barely need the tic.

### 17.3 Worked demonstration
One event — a daughter married into another house — told by three chroniclers. Same facts. Nothing marked, and no reader would confuse them.

**The soldier's widow.** *(Attention: bodies, weather, the quality of equipment. Silence: her own grief.)*
> *(original)* She went in the spring, and the road was dry, which was the only mercy in it. Their people sent four men and a cart. I have seen better carts on a battlefield. She did not cry until the gate, and then she did it properly, once, and got up into the cart.

**The bought scholar.** *(Attention: terms, documents, precedence. Silence: anything that would embarrass the house that pays him.)*
> *(original)* The match was concluded in the fourth month, upon terms that will bear examination. Three generations of maternal record were furnished by the other party and, in the judgement of this house, two of them are honest. The girl was seventeen. It is recorded here that she consented. It is not recorded here what she was asked.

**The priest.** *(Attention: ritual, order, the soul. Silence: doubt — right up until it isn't.)*
> *(original)* God gave her to this house, and this house gave her onward, as is right and as is written. There was a blessing at the gate. I gave it. I have given it many times, and it has never once caught in my throat before.

Each teller distorts a different part of the truth, and each one's last sentence is the place their silence leaks. That leak is where the technique earns its keep: **give every narrator one thing they are trying not to say, and let the prose fail to hide it exactly once per passage.**

### 17.4 Rules
- **Never let a narrator be neutral.** A voice with no stake is a narrator with no reason to exist.
- **The reader adjudicates, not the text.** Two accounts disagree; nothing in your own voice says which is right.
- **Age the voices.** A teller from three hundred years earlier should be harder to read, not merely more formal — shorter, blunter, less interested in interiority.
- **Cap the roster.** Four distinguishable voices is a lot. Beyond that, readers stop tracking and the differentiation becomes noise.

## 18. Anti-Patterns

The prose fails in recognisable ways. Check for all of these:

| Failure | Symptom | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Purple drift** | Multi-clause sentences of adjectives; "shimmering," "ethereal," "cascading" | Replace vague ornament with one concrete noun and a physical verb |
| **Aphorism spam** | Every third paragraph ends in a folk saying | Keep one per 2–3 pages; make the rest observation, not wisdom |
| **Triad wallpaper** | Threes on every page | Reserve announced triads for openings, endings, ritual |
| **Flat rhythm** | All sentences 14–18 words | Insert a four-word sentence; cut a clause from another |
| **Explaining the landing** | The short punch sentence is followed by a sentence explaining it | Delete the explanation. Always |
| **Unearned landing** | A short final sentence carrying feeling the paragraph never built | Build the expansion, or land on a plain fact instead of a plain emotion |
| **The melancholy default** | Every passage wistful; nothing brisk, funny, or plain | The frame is elegiac; the tale is not. Rewrite it as if telling a friend in a warm room |
| **Faux-archaism** | *ere, mayhap, 'twas* | Modern plain English; carry age through content |
| **Visual-first description** | Rooms introduced by what they look like | Lead with sound, temperature, and smell |
| **Adverb clusters** | *slowly, carefully, quietly* in one paragraph | One survives; the others become verbs or beats |
| **Sourceless wisdom** | Narrator pontificating in his own voice | Attribute it to the mentor, a proverb, or a song |
| **Technical music writing** | Keys, chords, fingerings | Describe cost, room, and one listener instead |
| **Glossary voice** | Invented term introduced by apposition: *a drab, the smallest coin* | Gloss by consequence (§14.2) |
| **Compound-image inflation** | Every paragraph closes on a cut-flower construction | Two or three per chapter (§6.4) |
| **Quirk without cost** | A charming, cryptic character being charming and cryptic to no end | Give the quirk a price, and let someone pay it on the page |
| **Variable-broken rhythm** | Templated text that limps on some substitutions | Keep variables out of landings and triads (§16) |
| **Assembled flatness** | Text stitched sentence-by-sentence; uniform length profile | Author whole paragraphs (§16.9) |

## 19. Worked Transformations

**Generic fantasy → Rothfuss (frame voice)**

> Before: *The inn was quiet that evening. The innkeeper, a red-haired man with a mysterious past, cleaned glasses behind the bar while two customers drank in the corner and discussed the troubling news from the east.*

> After *(original)*: *It was quiet in the Waystone, and it was the wrong kind of quiet. Two men sat at the end of the bar and drank with the steadiness of men avoiding a subject. Neither of them mentioned the roads. Behind the bar the innkeeper worked a cloth over a glass that had been clean for some time, and he did not hurry, and he did not stop.*

Note what changed: an absence framing, a triad of clauses, the negative construction, the removal of the character's exposition ("mysterious past"), and a landing built from *and*-chaining.

---

**Generic action → Rothfuss (telling voice)**

> Before: *I quickly realized I was in serious trouble. I had no money left and the tuition deadline was approaching rapidly, so I decided desperately to gamble on borrowing from a moneylender despite the enormous risk involved.*

> After *(original)*: *I counted my money twice, which is what you do when you already know the answer. Three jots and a drab. Term fees were due in nine days. There are people you can go to when your arithmetic comes out like that, and every one of them is expensive in a different way. I picked the one who would only want money.*

Note: adverbs deleted, abstraction replaced with a specific count, a rueful direct-address aside, a triad-adjacent rhythm, and a short landing that implies the alternatives without naming them.

---

**Generic description → Rothfuss (sensory hierarchy)**

> Before: *The market was busy and colourful, full of vendors selling all kinds of exotic goods under bright awnings.*

> After *(original)*: *You could hear the market three streets off, a low churn of voices with the fishmongers' cries riding on top of it. It smelled of hot fat, cut cedar, and the river. I had two pennies, and I spent an hour deciding how to lose them.*

---

**Templated text → variation-safe (see §16)**

> Before: *{Heir} felt nervous as {he/she} entered the hall, because {rival} had already claimed the high seat, which was worth {n} crowns a year in precedence.*

> After *(original)*: *The hall was colder than the yard had been, which told him how long they had let the fire go out. {Heir} took the seat nobody wanted. Across the table, {rival} was already eating, and did not look up, and that was the whole of the message.*

Note: the stated emotion is gone, replaced by a temperature and an action; the number is gone entirely; both variables sit early in short fixed frames; the landing is fixed text and survives any substitution.

## 20. The Revision Pass

Order matters. Do these one at a time, in sequence, because each pass changes what the next pass can see. Passes 2, 3, and 8 do most of the work.

1. **Read it aloud, whole.** Mark every place your voice stumbles. Fix nothing yet.
2. **Delete the first sentence of every paragraph.** Read each paragraph without it. Restore the ones that got worse — roughly two in three. The rest were throat-clearing.
3. **Hunt the landings.** Find every short punch sentence and read what follows it. If the next sentence explains, softens, or restates it, delete the next sentence.
4. **Profile the lengths.** Write each sentence's word count in the margin of a paragraph. A flat profile is a defect. Fix it by *cutting a clause*, never by adding one.
5. **Adverb sweep.** Every *-ly* gets challenged. Most become verbs or physical beats. A few survive on rhythm; make yourself say why.
6. **Sense audit.** For every new location, check that the first concrete detail is sound, temperature, or smell.
7. **Count the aphorisms and the compound images.** More than one aphorism per two pages, or more than three compound images per chapter: cut the weakest.
8. **Cut ten percent by word count.** Not by deleting a scene — by deleting words inside sentences you are keeping.
9. **Rest.** Weeks if you have them, days if you don't. Then run 1–8 again. The second pass finds structural problems the first pass hid, which is the entire reason Rothfuss applies the ten-percent cut repeatedly rather than once.

For assembled or templated text, add:

10. **Substitution sweep.** Render each template with its shortest and longest legal values and read both aloud. Any template that only works on the average value gets restructured (§16.3).

## 21. Drill Set

Practise these until the rhythms are automatic.

1. **The absence paragraph.** Describe an empty room using only what is not in it. Five sentences. End on four words or fewer.
2. **The announced triad.** "There were three things wrong with ___." Deliver three, ascending in weight, the third being emotional rather than physical.
3. **The music scene.** 200 words describing someone playing badly, then well. No musical terminology. Track one listener's face.
4. **The rueful aside.** Have the narrator describe a decision he made at sixteen, then judge it in one sentence without self-pity.
5. **The cost sentence.** Describe a magical act entirely through what it took out of the body performing it.
6. **The compound image.** Build three original phrases on the *cut-flower* pattern: an abstract state fused to a concrete object with a built-in fate.
7. **The silence beat.** Write an exchange where the emotionally important line is never spoken, and the answer is an action.
8. **The compression.** Summarise six months in three sentences, then render the next ninety seconds in three hundred words.
9. **The joke that isn't one.** Write a funny paragraph in which nothing is described as funny and nobody laughs. Land it in four words or fewer.
10. **The unglossed term.** Introduce an invented word three times across three short paragraphs, teaching it entirely by consequence, never defining it.
11. **The hundred words.** One passage, one technique, one landing, under 120 words. Then cut its first sentence and check whether it improved.
12. **The three tellers.** Write one event three times, from three chroniclers, changing what each notices and what each refuses to say. Do not change the facts.

## 22. Pre-Submission Checklist

- [ ] Read aloud. Anything that trips is wrong.
- [ ] Sentence-length profile jagged, not flat.
- [ ] Each significant paragraph ends on a short sentence, unexplained — and the expansion earned it.
- [ ] New locations introduced by sound/temperature/smell before sight.
- [ ] Adverb count low; every survivor justified by rhythm.
- [ ] No archaisms; vocabulary predominantly short and concrete.
- [ ] At most one aphorism per two to three pages; no more than three compound images per chapter.
- [ ] Announced triads confined to structurally important moments.
- [ ] Similes use homely, tactile vehicles.
- [ ] Dialogue tags are *said*; emotion carried by beats.
- [ ] Frame passages measurably quieter and slower than tale passages.
- [ ] Money is specific wherever it appears.
- [ ] The narrator has admitted, at least once in the chapter, to shaping the telling.
- [ ] Something in this chapter was funny, unless the chapter earned its solemnity.
- [ ] Invented terms introduced by use, not by definition; no more than three unglossed terms live at once.
- [ ] The first sentence is the right first sentence — verified by deleting it.

**For assembled or templated text, additionally:**

- [ ] No landing sentence contains a substituted variable.
- [ ] No variable inside a triad, and none in the last three words of a paragraph.
- [ ] Every template read aloud at its shortest and longest substitution.
- [ ] No sentence comments on the magnitude of a generated number.
- [ ] The unit of authorship is the paragraph.
